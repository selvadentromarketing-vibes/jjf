## What the fideicomiso is

A fideicomiso is a contract with a Mexican bank authorised to act as trustee. The bank holds formal title to the property and you are the beneficiary, with every right that matters: to use it, rent it, sell it and leave it to your heirs. It is how a foreign buyer acquires property in the coastal strip, and it is how our clients who are not Mexican buy in Tulum.

Put plainly: the bank's name is on the deed, and the decisions about the property are yours. The bank does not live in it, rent it or sell it on its own account. It acts on your instructions as beneficiary.

## Why it exists

Mexican law reserves the coastal strip, and Tulum sits inside it. The fideicomiso is the mechanism the law itself provides to settle that reservation without closing the door on a buyer from abroad. It is not a loophole and not a recent invention. It is the ordinary, expected route, the one the notary, the bank and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs work through every day.

That is worth saying clearly, because it is the question we are asked most often, and nearly always with the same worry behind it: that you are buying something borrowed. You are not.

## What it is not

It is not a lease. It is not a temporary permit or a concession anyone can withdraw from you. It is not shared ownership with the bank: the bank shares neither the use nor the decision.

Nor is it an exception granted to certain buyers. Every foreign buyer in the coastal strip buys this way, and a serious developer already has the arrangement with a trustee bank in place when it explains this to you.

## How long it lasts, and what comes after

It is set up for a long term and it is renewable. It is inherited. And it can be assigned when you sell: the trust passes to your buyer, or your buyer sets up their own, and that is settled inside the same transaction.

Those three things together, a long term, renewable, inheritable, are why the fideicomiso works for someone buying with their children in mind rather than a short cycle.

## What it costs

The bank charges to set the trust up and an annual fee to administer it. On top of that there are closing costs, which usually include the notary's fees, the state acquisition tax, registry duties and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs permit.

And there are costs that recur every year you own the property: the municipal property tax, the annual trust fee and the development's maintenance fee.

We do not publish the figures here, because they change over time, with the bank, and with the value of the property. We give you a written estimate for your own case before you sign anything, and we share the current figures from the bank we work with when we talk.

## Where you do not need one

Inland in Yucatán the coastal strip does not apply. In Izamal or Mérida you can buy directly or through a Mexican company, with no trust involved. That is the case at Hacienda Sacalá and at the Yucatán Country Club.

And if you are Mexican, you buy directly before a notary in either place. The rest of the process, notary, deed, registry, taxes, is the same.

This difference matters more than it looks when you are weighing the coast against the interior: it does not change your rights, but it changes the paperwork, the closing cost and the time. If you are still deciding where, start with [Tulum, the Riviera Maya or Mérida](/en/guides/tulum-riviera-maya-o-merida/).

## Who is involved

The notary is a lawyer with public faith, appointed by the State. The notary verifies title, gives the sale its legal form, calculates and withholds taxes and records the deed. You choose the notary; we can recommend those who know our developments.

The trustee bank sets up and administers the trust. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs issues the corresponding permit. The developer, us, hands over land with its legal history in order and its permits current, and puts the file on the table before you sign.

Your tax adviser, in Mexico and at home, is the person who can tell you what suits your particular case. It is worth consulting them before you buy, not after.

## If you are not in Mexico

The trust and the deed can be signed through a power of attorney, and viewings can be done over video. The whole purchase can be made at a distance.

Even so, nearly all our buyers come once or twice before deciding, and we would rather you came. The jungle at Tulum and the country in Yucatán do not travel well through a screen.

## How long it takes

A reservation is signed in days. Setting up a trust and closing the deed takes weeks, depending on the bank and the notary. We give you a realistic calendar at the start and tell you when something moves.

## When you sell

You will pay income tax on the gain, with deductions and, in some cases, exemptions for residents. The trust is assigned to your buyer, or a new one is set up in their name.

This is where a good tax adviser earns their keep, and it is why we press you to consult one before buying: almost everything that reduces the tax when you sell is decided at the moment you buy.

## Questions

### What is a fideicomiso?
It is a contract with an authorised Mexican bank under which the bank holds formal title to the property and you are the beneficiary, with the right to use, rent, sell and bequeath it. It is how a foreign buyer purchases in Mexico's coastal strip, where Tulum is.

### Does the bank own my house?
The bank holds formal title; the rights to use, rent, sell and bequeath are yours. It does not live in the property, rent it or sell it on its own account. It acts on your instructions as beneficiary.

### How long does a fideicomiso last?
It is set up for a long term and is renewable. It is inherited and can be assigned when you sell. That is why it works for a buyer thinking in the long term.

### Do I need Mexican residency to hold a fideicomiso?
No. You can buy with your passport and a tourist visa. Residency is a separate decision with its own benefits, and we can put you in touch with the people who arrange it.

### Where is a fideicomiso not needed?
Inland in Yucatán, in Izamal or Mérida, where you can buy directly or through a Mexican company. Also if you are Mexican: you buy directly before a notary anywhere in the country.

### What does it cost to open and hold a fideicomiso?
The bank charges for setting it up and an annual administration fee. Separately there are closing costs, notary, acquisition tax, registry and the Foreign Affairs permit, and the annual property tax and maintenance. We give you the figures in writing for your case, because they change with the bank and over time.
